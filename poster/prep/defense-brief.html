Most of the poster is solid and the story is good. These are the points where a sharp reviewer can catch you, in order of risk. Know what you will say for each.

High riskThe two India plants are not 14 MW and 1.4 MW
In the public Kaggle data both plants have 22 inverters and similar AC peaks: 29.2 MW (Plant 1) and 26.0 MW (Plant 2). Say this at the boardThe 60-second pitch

New solar plants have no power history, so we cannot train a forecaster for them. The obvious fix is to borrow a model from a mature plant, but if the two plants' power is on different scales, a model trained on one gives nonsense on the other: R² of −269.8.

We fix that in three steps: scale power by plant capacity (per-unit), remove the predictable daily sun curve with the Clear-Sky Index, and use a conditional domain adversarial network so the model's internal features look the same for both plants. It never sees a single power reading from the new plant. Within India that recovers R² of about 0.77 to 0.83.

Then we push it across the equator to Melbourne, and the alignment breaks: CDAN goes negative, while a plain Transformer keeps R² ≈ 0.54. So the real message is in the title: knowing when to transfer matters as much as how.

Section 1Problem and setting

Know this

  • Cold start: a newly commissioned plant has weather sensors but no power history to train on.
  • Zero target labels: during training the model sees source inputs and power, plus target inputs only. Target power is used only to score the result.
  • Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA): learn features that predict source power well and that a discriminator cannot tell apart between plants.
  • No fine-tuning: once trained, the model is applied to the target as is.
  • Evaluation is transductive: the unlabeled target inputs used during adaptation are the same period that is scored. This is standard for UDA benchmarks, but be ready to say it.

Likely questions

Why not just fine-tune on a few weeks of target data?

That works once data exists, and we list it as future work. Our case is day one, before any power records. Fine-tuning needs labels we do not have yet.

Is it realistic to have target weather but no target power?

Yes. Irradiance and temperature come from the site's weather station, a nearby station, satellite irradiance or a weather forecast, all available before the plant has a history.

Why not use a physical PV model (for example PVWatts) instead?

A physical model needs detailed system specs (tilt, azimuth, module and inverter data, losses) and still misses site effects. Our approach needs only capacity and weather. Comparing against a physical baseline is a fair suggestion; we have not run it.

If pushed: "Agreed, a physics baseline is the next comparison we should add."

What does "zero-target-label" mean exactly in your training loop?

Each training step takes a source batch (inputs and power) and a target batch (inputs only). The task loss uses source power only; the domain loss uses both batches. Target power never enters a gradient.

Know this

  • Inputs (7): irradiance (GHI), ambient temperature, module temperature, module minus ambient (ΔT), sin and cos of hour of day, Clear-Sky Index.
  • Target: plant DC power, summed over inverters. Window: 48 steps (12 hours).
  • Tasks: 1 = Plant 1 to Plant 2 (same site and days, scale mismatch); 2 = Plant 1 to Melbourne; 3 = Plant 2 to Melbourne.
  • Melbourne module temperature is estimated as ambient + 25 °C × GHI / 800 W/m², because the dataset has no module sensor. The paper implies it was measured.
  • Plant 2 outages explain part of the Task 1 error: a weather-driven model predicts what the sun allows, not when inverters are down.

Likely questions

Are Plant 1 and Plant 2 really 14 MW and 1.4 MW?

No. Both have 22 inverters and similar AC output (29 MW and 26 MW). Plant 1's DC column is recorded at about ten times its AC, which creates the 10× gap in the signal we trained on.

If pushed: "Correct, the capacities in the paper need fixing. The method treats the gap as a scale mismatch, and the result that scale must be decoupled still holds. We want to repeat it on plants of genuinely different size."

Is 34 days of data enough?

It is enough to show the scale failure and the alignment gain within one site and season, which is what Task 1 tests. It is not enough to learn seasons, and that is part of why cross-hemisphere transfer breaks. A year or more of source data is the obvious next step.

How did you handle the Melbourne data?

We merged three UNISOLAR tables (generation for Site 25, campus weather, Bundoora irradiance), converted irradiance timestamps from UTC (+10 h), resampled to 15 minutes, converted kWh per 15 minutes to kW (×4), and estimated module temperature from ambient temperature and irradiance.

How did you avoid data leakage?

Target power is never used in training. Feature scalers are fit on the source and reused unchanged on the target.

Know: the paper says the scalers were fit on the first 80% of the source; the shared code fits them on all source data and has no validation split. If asked about model selection: "fixed 60 epochs, no early stopping on target data."

Section 3Method

Know this

  • Per-unit scaling: divide power by capacity so the model learns a 0 to 1 shape, then multiply by the target's capacity at the end. In code, Plant 2 is multiplied by the fixed ratio 10, and Melbourne predictions are scaled by 384.12 kW divided by Plant 1's observed peak.
  • Clear-Sky Index (Haurwitz): clear-sky irradiance = 1098 · cos(zenith) · exp(−0.057 / cos(zenith)). CSI = measured GHI / clear-sky GHI. It removes the predictable sun arc and leaves the cloud effect, which transfers better between sites.
  • Backbones: 2-layer LSTM; Transformer (2 layers, 4 heads, d = 64); PatchTST (patches of 8 steps).
  • DANN: a discriminator guesses which plant a feature came from; the Gradient Reversal Layer (GRL) flips its gradient so the encoder learns to fool it.
  • CDAN: the discriminator sees the features combined with the prediction (outer product), so alignment is done per output regime instead of overall.
  • How CDAN handles regression here: the head first outputs a 4-way softmax (soft "generation regimes"), then a linear layer turns it into power. The discriminator is conditioned on features ⊗ that 4-way vector.
  • λ schedule: 2 / (1 + e−10p) − 1, rising from 0 to 1 over training so alignment starts gently. Domain loss weight 0.5.
  • Training: Adam, learning rate 1e-3, weight decay 1e-4, cosine schedule, 60 epochs, batch 64, gradient clipping 1.0.
  • Hard-zero gating: if GHI ≤ 0.005 kW/m² the prediction is set to 0. Applied to all six models, so it is fair.

Likely questions

CDAN was designed for classification. How do you condition on a regression output?

The task head predicts a 4-way softmax, a soft assignment to generation regimes, and power is a linear read-out of it. The discriminator is conditioned on features ⊗ that softmax, so it compares source and target within similar output regimes.

What does the Gradient Reversal Layer do, and where is it?

Forward it does nothing; backward it multiplies the gradient by −λ, so the encoder works against the discriminator. We apply it to the features before the outer product. Placing it after the outer product pushed reversed gradients into the regression head and cost us about 0.2 R² (see "What we ran into").

Isn't zero-gating at night cheating?

No. A PV plant produces nothing without light; 5 W/m² is about the sensor noise floor. It is applied identically to every model, so the comparison stays fair. Its job is to remove night predictions caused by warm nights (about 25 °C).

Why Haurwitz and not a more detailed clear-sky model like Ineichen?

Haurwitz needs only the solar zenith angle, no turbidity or altitude data, which suits a cold-start plant. In the code the clear-sky curve is also rescaled to each site's 99th-percentile irradiance before dividing. A better clear-sky model is a reasonable upgrade.

Why a 12-hour window?

It covers the morning ramp up to the current time, so the model sees how the day has developed. We did not tune it.

Is this forecasting or estimation?

The model maps a 12-hour weather history, including the current step, to current power. In deployment the current-step weather comes from a weather forecast, so it becomes a forecast. We tested with measured weather.

Within India (Task 1)

  • Every CDAN model jumps from about 0.53–0.55 to 0.77–0.79. Conditional alignment is the biggest single gain.
  • DANN barely helps (0.5295). Matching the overall feature distribution is not enough; matching per regime is.
  • The backbone matters less than the alignment: LSTM, Transformer and PatchTST all land within 0.02 of each other with CDAN.
  • MAE of 1,284 kW sounds large but Plant 2 peaks near 26 MW, so it is about 5% of peak.

Across the equator (Tasks 2 and 3)

  • CDAN + LSTM and the proposed model go negative.
  • The unadapted Transformer is the most consistent: 0.5428 and 0.4325.
  • DANN is best on Task 2 but negative on Task 3: adversarial methods are unstable here.
  • Source data is 34 hot days; the target is a full Melbourne year. Much of the target is out of range for the source.

Why CDAN flattens

The CDAN head predicts power as a linear combination of a 4-way softmax, so its output can never go above its largest bin value. On Task 1 that caps the peaks. Across the equator the adversarial pressure pushes the softmax into one bin, so the model outputs the same value all day and the gate turns it into a square wave. This is a sharper, data-backed explanation for the negative R² than the season story, and it is something you can point at.

Likely questions

Why did CDAN fail across hemispheres?

Two reasons we can show. First, the source is 34 hot days in India while the target spans every Melbourne season, so much of the target is outside what the source saw, and forcing alignment distorts the features. Second, the CDAN head collapses to one regime and outputs a near-constant value (see the output plot). Seasonal phase mismatch is our working hypothesis for why alignment goes wrong, not a verified mechanism.

Why did the plain Transformer do better across hemispheres?

It has no alignment term pulling target features toward the wrong source states, so it simply maps irradiance and temperature to power. It still overshoots (the scale is off), but its shape is right, so R² stays positive.

Avoid claiming "attention learns universal physics". Keep it to "no alignment, so no negative transfer".

DANN was best on Task 2. Why do you say the Transformer is the robust choice?

Because DANN is negative on Task 3 (−0.1579), while the Transformer is positive on both (0.5428, 0.4325). We value consistency for a plant we cannot check. Admit the poster headline overgeneralizes.

Is 0.8325 really better than 0.7923? Did you run several seeds?

Single runs, no seeds. The gaps among the top Task 1 models (0.77 to 0.83) are probably within run-to-run variation. The robust finding is the jump from about 0.53 without conditional alignment to about 0.77 or more with it.

Where does R² = −269.8 come from?

Without scaling, the model predicts on the source's 10× larger scale. The squared errors become hundreds of times the target's variance, so R² = 1 − MSE / Var(y) goes far below zero.

What does the ablation show?

Each piece adds skill: per-unit scaling gets you from −269.8 to 0.58, CDAN to 0.69, time encoding to 0.80, CSI to 0.83. Scaling is by far the biggest step.

Know: this ablation is not in the shared results files; it came from the earlier experiments.

Why is MAE in kW so different between tasks?

Plant 2 peaks near 26 MW, Melbourne is 384 kW. Compare R² across tasks, and MAE only within a task.

Section 5What we ran into

  1. Raw transfer collapsed (R² ≈ −270). The model reproduced the source's 10× larger scale on the target. Fix: per-unit scaling, which alone recovers R² ≈ 0.58.
  2. Warm nights produced fake power. Unconstrained models predicted 50 to 200 kW at 2 AM because night temperatures stayed near 25 °C. Fix: hard-zero gating below 5 W/m², applied to every model.
  3. A GRL placement bug. R² dropped from 0.83 (exp_07) to 0.63 (exp_09) after the reversal layer was moved onto the outer product, which pushed reversed gradients into the regression head. Moving it back onto the features, plus cosine learning rate and weight decay, restored 0.77 to 0.79 (exp_10).
  4. The Australia surprise. The same alignment that helped in India went negative in Melbourne. That changed the paper's message from "our method wins" to "learn when to transfer".
  5. Data plumbing. Different date formats between the two Kaggle plants, three separate UNISOLAR tables with a UTC offset, kWh-to-kW conversion, and no module temperature sensor in Melbourne.
  6. The story evolved. An earlier draft was titled "The Domain Adaptation Paradox" with CDAN + Transformer (0.7923) as the headline; the final paper headlines the full CSI model (0.8325).

Likely questions

What was the hardest part?

Making adversarial training stable for regression: the GRL placement bug cost about 0.2 R² and took gradient tracing to find. The second was accepting that the method fails across hemispheres and turning that into a finding.

What would you do differently?

Use at least a full year of source data, plants of genuinely different size, several random seeds with error bars, a held-out validation split, and a physical PV baseline.

Section 7Limits and future work

Admit freely

  • Three plants, two regions, 34 days of India data.
  • Single runs, no seeds or confidence intervals.
  • Measured weather as input (perfect-forecast setting).
  • The cross-hemisphere mechanism is a hypothesis.
  • No physical-model or fine-tuning baseline yet.

Next steps to name

  • Fine-tune once the first weeks of target power arrive.
  • Multi-year source data and plants of truly different size.
  • A "transfer risk" check that warns before aligning dissimilar climates.
  • Real weather forecasts as inputs; seeds and error bars.
  • Head-to-head runs against published transfer pipelines.

Likely questions

How would an operator know whether to use adaptation for a new plant?

Compare the target's weather statistics with the source's. If they overlap (same region and season), use CDAN; if not, use the unadapted Transformer. Turning that into an automatic check is our next step, and it is what the title means.

What is the practical takeaway for a grid operator?

Always normalize by capacity. Use alignment only between similar climates. Across very different climates, a plain model is the safer default until local data arrives.
